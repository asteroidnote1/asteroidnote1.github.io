Is B Really Better?

A decision guide for LLM evals — honest intervals, paired comparisons, and how many cases you actually need

32 passes out of 40 looks like 0.80. The honest 95% range is 0.65 to 0.90. Was your last "improvement" inside that range?

The problem

You changed a prompt, a model, or your retrieval step. The eval score went up. Then someone asks: "Is B really better, or did we get lucky?"

  • You rerun the eval and the score moves.
  • The LLM judge doesn't fully agree with itself, or with you.
  • Many of your cases come from the same few documents.
  • Nobody agreed beforehand what "better" means, or how sure you need to be.

So the ship decision comes down to gut feeling, or to whoever argues longest.

Computing a statistic is the easy part, and free libraries already do it. The hard part is knowing which question you're asking, which number answers it, how many cases you need, and how to write it up so a non-statistician can make the call.

What the guide helps you do

After reading it, you can:

  1. Put an honest interval on a pass/fail or graded eval score.
  2. Decide whether B beats A on the same cases, with a stated error rate.
  3. Work out how many cases you need before you build them.
  4. Report the result on one page that a non-statistician can act on.

Who it's for

  • Engineers, PMs, and tech leads who own an eval set of roughly 30–2,000 cases. The main focus is pass/fail scores; graded scores are covered more briefly.
  • People who must decide whether a prompt, model, or retrieval change is a real improvement.
  • Anyone who can run a Python script. No statistics background assumed.

Who it's not for

  • Learning to design evals from scratch.
  • Benchmark research that needs item response theory or hierarchical Bayesian models.
  • Step-by-step tutorials for a specific vendor's eval tool.
  • Live-traffic A/B testing. The guide includes only a short note on this.

The shipornot command-line tool (version 0.1.0, with unit tests, MIT license) is already free at https://github.com/asteroidnote1/shipornot. The guide adds:

  • The decision procedure in the chapters above: which command to run, when, and what to conclude.
  • The simulation scripts and saved results behind the verification report.
  • A CI regression-gate script.
  • Synthetic example datasets with a walkthrough.
  • A calculator workbook for the pass/fail calculations (intervals, the paired comparison, sample size, MDE, and the gate). Bootstrap and simulation need Python with shipornot.
  • Checklists and a one-page report template.

The procedure also works with any tool that exports per-case results as CSV.
